§ 02 — Learning Goals & Reflections

What I Set Out to Learn

Going into this term, I had a clear sense of what I wanted to get out of it. This was my fourth work term, and I wanted to use that experience intentionally — to fill specific gaps I had noticed in earlier placements rather than just showing up and doing what I was asked.

Goal 01 · Critical & Creative Thinking
Getting better at solving problems I hadn't seen before
One thing I noticed in previous terms was that I was good at solving problems I already understood, but I slowed down a lot when something was genuinely new. At Ciena, urgent requests came in constantly — often with little context and no clear playbook. My goal was to get more comfortable sitting with that ambiguity and working through it systematically, rather than waiting for someone to point me in the right direction. I also wanted to stop jumping to the first solution that came to mind and actually consider alternatives before committing.
✦ Did I achieve it?
Yes. By the end of the term I was handling urgent requests that I would have escalated immediately at the start. Troubleshooting real hardware failures daily forced me to build a more structured way of thinking through problems, and I got a lot faster at it. Colleagues started coming to me with questions, which felt like a meaningful signal.
Goal 02 · Time Management & Professionalism
Staying organized when everything feels urgent
Production support environments don't give you the luxury of focusing on one thing at a time. I wanted to get better at triaging — figuring out what actually needed my attention right now versus what could wait — without dropping anything. I also wanted to get more disciplined about communicating proactively when I was blocked or running behind, instead of going quiet and hoping I'd figure it out.
✦ Did I achieve it?
Mostly yes. I got noticeably better at managing parallel tasks and meeting deadlines even during high-volume weeks. The one area I'm still working on is sending status updates earlier rather than waiting until I have something concrete to report — that's something I want to bring into my next term.
Goal 03 · Written Communication
Writing documentation that actually gets read
Technical writing was something I had always treated as an afterthought. My goal this term was to treat it as part of the work itself — writing incident reports, test summaries, and internal docs that people could actually understand and act on without having to ask me follow-up questions. I wanted to practice adapting the same information for different readers, since some colleagues wanted the technical details and others just needed to know the outcome.
✦ Did I achieve it?
Yes. The number of follow-up questions I received after writing a report dropped over the course of the term, which I took as a sign that the writing was getting clearer. I also got direct feedback from my supervisor that my documentation had improved. This is a skill I'll carry into every future role.
Goal 04 · Global Understanding
Learning how to work with people in different time zones and contexts
I knew going in that Ciena operated across multiple continents, and I wanted to use that as an opportunity rather than just something to work around. My goal was to understand how working across time zones actually changes the way you communicate and plan — not just logistically, but in terms of expectations and norms. I also wanted to get better at collaborating with people whose technical backgrounds or communication styles were different from mine.
✦ Did I achieve it?
Yes. Coordinating urgent requests with engineers in different regions pushed me to be more intentional about documentation and async communication — since you can't always just Slack someone and get an answer in five minutes. I came away with a much better sense of how distributed teams actually function, and what it takes to keep things moving across time zones.
Goal 05 · Technological Literacy
Building real fluency with production tools and systems
I wanted to go beyond surface-level familiarity with the tools I was using. In previous terms I sometimes felt like I was just following steps without fully understanding why they worked. This term I set a goal to actually understand the systems I was touching — the test equipment, the automation infrastructure, and how hardware and software interact in an optical component context — well enough to explain them to someone else and troubleshoot when things broke unexpectedly.
✦ Did I achieve it?
Yes. By the end of the term I was independently debugging equipment issues and building automation tools without needing to look everything up or ask for help at every step. The breadth of products I worked on also forced me to pick things up quickly, which is a habit I want to keep developing going forward.

§ 03 — Job Description

Optical Component Test Engineer

As an Optical Component Test Engineer on Ciena's production support team, no two days looked alike. My primary responsibility was handling urgent requests from engineers, labs, and operations teams spread across multiple continents — troubleshooting failures in optical modules, transceivers, and line cards under real production constraints.


A major focus of the role was automation: I designed and built tooling that bridged hardware test equipment with software systems, reducing manual effort on repetitive test sequences and freeing engineers to focus on higher-level analysis. Beyond coding, I was a first-line collaborator with colleagues in Asia-Pacific, Europe, and North America — coordinating across time zones to unblock critical workflows and keep production moving.


Every day brought a different product, a different failure mode, and a different person on the other end of a support ticket. That variety pushed me to build both deep technical knowledge and the communication skills to translate findings quickly for engineers who needed answers fast.

§ 04 — Conclusion

Takeaways

My fourth co-op term at Ciena was the most demanding — and the most rewarding — of my undergraduate career. I entered as a software student and left as someone who can speak fluently across hardware, firmware, and software boundaries. The urgency of production support compressed years of potential learning into a single semester: I shipped automation that real engineers relied on daily, collaborated with colleagues on every continent, and learned that the clearest mark of competence is how quickly you can make someone else's problem your own.


If you take one thing from this report, let it be this: global infrastructure runs on people — on the trust between a test engineer in Ottawa and a lab technician in Japan at 2 a.m. Ciena gave me the privilege of being one of those people, and that experience will shape how I approach every technical challenge that comes next.
